  Working on the Cube Craze project gave me a much better understanding of how much of engineering design comes down to iteration, testing, and making practical tradeoffs under time constraints. My main contributions were in coding, troubleshooting, testing, assembly, and helping refine the robot’s overall strategy. I worked on developing and adjusting the control logic that governed the robot’s opening movement sequence and later adaptive roaming behavior, with the goal of making the robot both reliable and effective in competition. A large part of that process was not simply writing code once, but repeatedly testing it on the board, identifying where behavior broke down, and making small changes to timings, turning behavior, and edge response until the robot performed consistently. I was also heavily involved in hands-on assembly and troubleshooting, which meant working through wiring issues, mechanical inconsistencies, and the many small problems that only appear once a design is tested in real conditions rather than on paper.

  One of the most valuable parts of the project was seeing how strategy and implementation had to evolve together. Early ideas were more ambitious mechanically, but through testing we realized that a simpler and more robust robot would likely perform better than a more complicated design with more failure points. I contributed to that shift by helping evaluate what was actually working during milestone testing and competition preparation, and by focusing on making the final system dependable rather than overcomplicated. In the end, I think our robot performed well because it was built around repeatable behavior, straightforward collection, and a strategy that took advantage of early cube acquisition and continued movement after the initial sequence. This project reinforced for me that effective engineering is often less about building the flashiest solution and more about building something that can be tested, trusted, and improved under real constraints.
